### Cycle 1 / Cell 01 / Focal mark

Differentiation × Focal × Standing

Dialogue

In a kitchen, two housemates are sorting through a drawer of spare keys.

Nina: Is one of these for the back shed?

Tom: I thought the little silver one was.

Nina: There are three little silver ones.

Tom: Oh. I didn't realise they looked so alike.

Nina: This one's got a round head. The other two are more square.

Tom: The round one might be for the side gate.

Nina: Do you remember anything about the shed key?

Tom: I remember it had something odd near the teeth. I thought it was a chip.

Nina: A chip in the metal?

Tom: Yes, right at the end.

Nina: Look at this one. Is that what you mean?

Tom: No, that's just where the metal's worn down. Turn it over.

Nina: There's a tiny triangle stamped on the other side.

Tom: That's it. I remember noticing that when we got the shed.

Nina: So this is the shed key?

Tom: Yes. The one with the triangle.

Nina: I'll put it on its own hook.

Tom: Good. I can finally tell which one it is.

Structural check

- Change: An indistinguishable key acquires a determinate identity within a collection of similar keys.
- Sufficiency: The identifying mark at one decisive locus resolves which particular key is meant. The mark establishes the identity rather than merely suggesting another way of searching.
- Persistence: The identified key remains available under the determination established in the conversation: the one with the stamped triangle.
- Closest rival cell and decisive difference: Cell 02, Binary bearing. The completion here does not consist in the key's continuing status as an exception relative to another key or an expectation. It consists in the establishment of this particular key's identity through its decisive mark.

Assessment: Target achieved.

### Cycle 1 / Cell 02 / Binary bearing

Differentiation × Focal × Bearing

Dialogue

Two colleagues are preparing name cards for a small dinner.

Ruth: I thought everyone was getting the same meal.

Eddie: That's what I was told. Chicken for all twelve.

Ruth: Then why does this card say vegetarian?

Eddie: Which one?

Ruth: Amira's.

Eddie: I thought she said she was happy with the chicken.

Ruth: No, I remember her saying she was happy with the menu, provided there was a vegetarian option.

Eddie: Wasn't there someone else who requested that too?

Ruth: I checked the replies this morning. Everyone else selected chicken.

Eddie: So she's the only one having something different?

Ruth: Yes, for the main course. Her starter's the same as everyone else's.

Eddie: Ah, I was reading the card as though her whole meal was different.

Ruth: No. It's just the main.

Eddie: Then the caterer needs eleven chicken mains and one vegetarian main.

Ruth: Exactly. Amira's the exception to the chicken order.

Eddie: I'll make sure they don't prepare twelve.

Structural check

- Change: Amira's meal, initially assimilated to the uniform order, acquires a determinate exceptional standing.
- Sufficiency: The focal distinction is constituted by the expectation that all twelve mains are chicken and by Amira's departure from that expectation. Her exceptional status is established precisely against the other eleven orders.
- Persistence: The distinction remains what it is through the contrast between her vegetarian main and the otherwise uniform chicken order. Without that comparison, the same vegetarian choice would not have the established status of an exception.
- Closest rival cell and decisive difference: Cell 01, Focal mark. Identifying a vegetarian meal by itself would establish an identity. Here, what is achieved is the status of one meal as different from the governing expectation.

Assessment: Target achieved.

### Cycle 1 / Cell 03 / Boundary act

Differentiation × Focal × Efficacy

Dialogue

Two friends are preparing to send birthday invitations through a community hall's booking system.

Leah: It won't let me select the smaller room.

Owen: Why not?

Leah: It says our party's too big.

Owen: How many have you put down?

Leah: Thirty.

Owen: That room holds thirty, doesn't it?

Leah: That's what I thought. Look at the listing.

Owen: It says maximum twenty-nine guests.

Leah: That's annoying. I was counting thirty because I'm including the baby.

Owen: Does the baby count as a guest?

Leah: I don't know. The form says to include everyone who'll be in the room.

Owen: Then probably, yes.

Leah: But if one person can't come, we're at twenty-nine.

Owen: And it would let us book?

Leah: Let's see. I'll make a test entry with twenty-nine. I'm not submitting it yet.

Owen: There. The small room's appeared.

Leah: Change it back to thirty.

Owen: Gone again.

Leah: So twenty-nine is the cutoff. At thirty it sends us to the larger room.

Owen: I'll ask Cara whether she's bringing her partner before we decide which room to book.

Leah: Yes. That answer determines which room we can use.

Structural check

- Change: A numerical difference that was initially treated as unimportant becomes an operative dividing point in the booking process.
- Sufficiency: The exact transition between twenty-nine and thirty guests determines which room the system permits. The participants establish the boundary by testing both sides of it.
- Persistence: The distinction persists as a functioning rule of the booking system. Subsequent guest counts receive different treatment depending on which side of the cutoff they fall.
- Closest rival cell and decisive difference: Cell 01, Focal mark. The completed achievement is not simply knowing the room's maximum occupancy. It is establishing the precise point at which a different booking response takes effect.

Assessment: Target achieved.

### Cycle 1 / Cell 04 / Scope standing

Differentiation × Distributed × Standing

Dialogue

Two volunteers are sorting donated books for a school fair.

Maeve: Can we just put all the children's books on these two tables?

Daniel: We could, but I think the little ones will have trouble finding anything.

Maeve: We haven't got enough tables to separate every age group.

Daniel: Maybe we don't need to. What kinds have we actually got?

Maeve: These are mostly picture books. Big pages, hardly any writing.

Daniel: And those thin ones?

Maeve: Beginning readers, I think. Short sentences, large print.

Daniel: This pile looks different again. They're still children's books, but they're proper stories with chapters.

Maeve: Some of the covers make it hard to tell. Look at this one with the dragon.

Daniel: I thought that was a picture book.

Maeve: Open it. Nearly two hundred pages, hardly any illustrations.

Daniel: Right. So covers aren't much help.

Maeve: What about these reference books? Animals, planets, that sort of thing.

Daniel: They're not really in the same sequence as the stories, are they?

Maeve: No. A child could be reading chapter books and still want one of those.

Daniel: So picture books, early readers, chapter books, and factual books?

Maeve: Let's check the last box before we settle on that.

Daniel: Mostly chapter books. A few more factual ones.

Maeve: Any that don't fit?

Daniel: Just this cookbook for children.

Maeve: Factual. It can go with the other activity and information books.

Daniel: That's the lot, then. Four sections.

Maeve: Yes. We can label the tables by section instead of pretending there's only one kind of children's book.

Structural check

- Change: A collection initially treated as a single undifferentiated category acquires an articulated pattern of differences.
- Sufficiency: The distinctions are established across the relevant donated collection, including potentially misleading covers and books that do not fit a simple reading-age progression. No isolated book or single contrast establishes the completed organization.
- Persistence: The differentiated collection stands as four established sections. Their arrangement remains available for the volunteers and visitors without requiring each book to be reconsidered individually.
- Closest rival cell and decisive difference: Cell 05, Context-bound mark. The distinctions here are established across the collection as an articulated account of its kinds. They do not depend on identifying which particular book becomes unusual when placed among different companions.

Assessment: Target achieved.

### Cycle 1 / Cell 05 / Context-bound mark

Differentiation × Distributed × Bearing

Dialogue

Two siblings are looking through photographs of their family's annual seaside trips.

Orla: Mum asked me to pick a photograph where Dad looks different from usual.

Finn: That shouldn't be hard. He looks different in half of these.

Orla: I was thinking of this one where he's wearing a suit.

Finn: But everyone was dressed up that day. It was Aunt Linda's wedding.

Orla: True. He doesn't stand out in that picture at all.

Finn: What about the beach photos?

Orla: He's wearing that ridiculous yellow hat in most of them.

Finn: Exactly. That's practically his normal beach look.

Orla: Look at this one from Galway. He's not wearing it.

Finn: He's got a plain cap on.

Orla: Which is odd for him at the beach, but if you put that photograph with his work pictures, he looks completely ordinary.

Finn: So are we looking for what makes him unusual, or what makes him look unlike the rest of the people in a photo?

Orla: Mum said she wanted a picture where he stood out. She didn't say how.

Finn: This one from the swimming club might work.

Orla: Why? They're all wearing bright swimming caps.

Finn: That's the point. Dad's the only one without one.

Orla: Oh, yes. But in the family beach pictures, not wearing a swimming cap wouldn't be unusual at all.

Finn: And in this photograph, it is.

Orla: Look at the Christmas swim. There he's wearing a bright cap, but among all those people in ordinary woolly hats, he's the odd one again.

Finn: So even the same thing can make him blend in with one group and stand out in another.

Orla: I think I'll show Mum both. They're funny together.

Finn: Good idea. She can choose which sort of unusual she meant.

Structural check

- Change: What counts as distinctive becomes determinate through the shifting composition of the groups being compared. The same clothing or absence of clothing carries different significance in different company.
- Sufficiency: The participants establish that distinctiveness is constituted by the comparison field: the swimming club, the family beach photographs, and the Christmas swimmers provide different standards of what stands out.
- Persistence: The achieved distinctions retain their identities through their respective contexts. Dad's appearance is unusual or ordinary only in relation to the particular company against which it is understood.
- Closest rival cell and decisive difference: Cell 04, Scope standing. Cell 04 establishes an articulated pattern of differences across a collection. Here, the achievement is determining how distinctiveness itself depends on the comparison group, so that changing the group changes what is distinctive.

Assessment: Target achieved.
